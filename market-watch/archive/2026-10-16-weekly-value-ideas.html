Friday, Oct 16, 2026 (Bangkok) · Warren for Tyler · Prices as of the Oct 15 US close

Tyler, I once watched a man spend twenty years panning a creek in the hills. He worked hard, bought new gear every time the price of gold went up, and gave most of it back every time it went down. The fellow who did best on that creek never touched a pan. He owned the land and took a small cut of every ounce that came out of it. This week's two ideas are both about being that landowner, and about not overpaying for the privilege.

Idea 1: Franco-Nevada, the toll road on the gold mine

The principle: a durable moat, then price versus value

A moat is whatever lets a business keep earning high returns on its capital while competitors try to take them away. But a wonderful business bought at a full price can still be a mediocre investment. Value is what the business will hand you over its life. Price is what Mr. Market is asking today. Margin of safety is the gap between the two, and it's there to cover the things you got wrong.

What it is and how it makes money

Franco-Nevada (ticker FNV, on both New York and Toronto) doesn't run mines. It pays miners cash up front and in exchange gets a royalty (a cut of revenue) or a stream (the right to buy metal at a fixed low price). The miner carries the payroll, the diesel, the cave-ins and the cost overruns. Franco-Nevada collects. The company describes itself as debt-free, using free cash flow to buy more royalties and pay dividends (Q2 2026 release).

Here's what that looked like in the second quarter, all from the company's Q2 2026 results (also filed with the SEC as Exhibit 99.1):

  • Revenue was $580.9 million, up 57% from a year earlier, and 132,405 gold equivalent ounces were sold, up 18%.
  • Adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) was $529.7 million, a 91.2% margin.
  • General and administrative expense for the whole quarter was $7.8 million. That's about 1.3% of revenue (my arithmetic).
  • Precious metals were 86% of revenue, and 88% of revenue came from the Americas.
  • The balance sheet held $1,014.2 million of cash against total liabilities of $681.5 million, with $4.3 billion of "available capital" counting equity stakes and undrawn credit lines.
  • The quarterly dividend is $0.44 a share.

Why it's a moat

Three things, in plain words. First, it has almost no exposure to mining cost inflation, because the operator pays the costs. Second, it's spread across many mines, metals, countries and operators, so one bad mine doesn't sink it. Third, and this is my read rather than a reported fact, its reputation and its cash pile make it a natural first call when a miner needs money. A 91% margin on a 1.3% overhead is what a toll road looks like on paper.

Who's behind it

CEO Paul Brink runs the company. In the Q2 release he pointed to "strong organic growth evidenced by resource increases, planned mine expansions and project advancements." That's the hidden kicker in this model. When an operator finds more ore on land Franco-Nevada already has a royalty on, Franco-Nevada gets the extra ounces without paying another dime. The same release lists several of these, such as IAMGOLD's updated resource at Côté Gold and Lundin's underground expansion plans at Candelaria.

Price versus value: where the margin of safety gets thin

FNV closed at $240.88 on Oct 15 (Yahoo Finance). Using about 193 million diluted shares from the Q2 report, the market values the company at roughly $46.5 billion. First-half adjusted net income was $4.19 a share. If you simply double that, which is my rough annualizing and not company guidance, you get about $8.38 a share, a price-to-earnings ratio near 29, and an earnings yield of about 3.5%. The dividend yields well under 1%.

Now the part folks skip. The company's 2026 guidance assumes gold at $4,500 an ounce. Spot gold was around $4,150 to $4,175 on Oct 15 (Ausecure close; goldpricetracker futures data), which is roughly 7 to 8% below that assumption. Gold's highest close this year was $5,513.20 on Jan 28 (StatMuse), so the metal is about a quarter off its peak. The first half's record earnings were earned at prices we don't have today.

So my judgment: this is a wonderful business at a full price. At about 29 times earnings that rested on $4,500 gold, you're paying for the moat and then some, and you're not being paid much for the risk that gold keeps sliding. This is a watchlist name, not a back-up-the-truck name. The work is to decide, before the market tests you, what you'd pay if gold sat at a conservative number for five years. Then wait for the price to come to you.

The bear case

  • Gold is a price, not a product. Buffett's 2011 letter put it best. All the world's gold, melded together, would form a cube about 68 feet per side, and "you can fondle the cube, but it will not respond." He said gold has two shortcomings, "being neither of much use nor procreative." Franco-Nevada is far better than a bar of metal because it reinvests and grows, but 70% of its Q2 revenue was gold, so its fortunes still rise and fall with that cube's price.
  • One big asset is still frozen. Cobre Panamá remains in "Preservation and Safe Management" with production halted. Panama let stockpiled ore be processed starting in 2026 and set up a ministerial commission on the mine's future. Expected stream deliveries from the stockpiles are about 23,100 gold ounces and 265,000 silver ounces in total, a small fraction of a full restart (Q2 release). Politics can take a moat away with a signature.
  • You don't control the mine. Results depend on other people's operations. The same release shows Equinox cutting Valentine's 2026 guidance and winter storms shutting Caserones for 13 days.
  • The fine print. The company's own risk factors list Canada Revenue Agency audits, possible changes to Canadian tax treatment of offshore streams, and a passive foreign investment company question for US holders.
  • Capital allocation risk. $4.3 billion of available capital is a strength until it's spent at the top of the cycle. Watch what they pay for the next big deal.

Bottom line: the right business, the wrong price for a big position today. In the long run, the landowner wins, but even the landowner can overpay for the land.

Idea 2: the small marketplace, judged like a business

The principle: a moat you can afford to build

You've raised small marketplace ventures, Tyler, and I want to give you a test to run before you spend a dollar. Bill Gurley, a venture investor at Benchmark (the firm behind eBay and Uber), wrote the clearest one I know: "All Markets Are Not Created Equal" (2012). A marketplace's moat is the network effect, which means the thousandth customer gets a better experience than the first one did. But a network effect only shows up in the right kind of market.

How it applies on a low budget

Here are the four of Gurley's ten factors I'd lean on hardest, because a small budget can't fix a market that fails them.

  1. High fragmentation on both sides. Gurley's point is that a concentrated supplier base will fight a new middleman and refuse to share the economics. He uses airlines squeezing online ticket sellers as the example. Pick a niche where both the sellers and the buyers are many and small.
  2. A real improvement over the status quo. His bar is a "wow" moment, as when OpenTable let you search hundreds of restaurants in seconds instead of phoning them one by one. If the buyer's alternative is already a decent Facebook group, you don't have one.
  3. Frequency. People who buy often learn to come straight to you. People who buy once a decade go back to Google every time.
  4. Being in the payment flow. If the money moves through you, you get paid for the value you create. If it doesn't, you're a free bulletin board.

The low-budget way to test this is to be the marketplace by hand before you build it. Match the first twenty buyers and sellers yourself through a spreadsheet and a group chat. If you can't get both sides to come back without software, software won't save it. That costs you weekends, not capital, and it protects you from ruin, which matters more than any single win.

The bear case

These points are my own judgment, not Gurley's.

  • The cold start. Neither side shows up until the other one does, and that chicken-and-egg problem kills most small marketplaces before the network effect ever kicks in.
  • Leakage. Once a buyer and seller meet, they can cut you out and deal directly next time. That's why being in the payment flow and adding real value after the match, like trust, logistics or guarantees, matter so much.
  • A small niche stays small. Fragmented and frequent beats big and empty, but you should know the ceiling before you fall in love.
  • Execution beats the checklist. Gurley warns that this analysis "could easily mislead one into underestimating the critical role that execution plays."

Bottom line: run the four-factor test on paper, then run it by hand for a month. Pass both, and you've earned the right to spend money.

The thread between them

Both ideas say the same thing in different clothes. Own the toll, not the pan. Then refuse to overpay for the toll, whether you're paying with dollars for a stock or with your weekends for a venture. Patience is cheap, and ruin is expensive.
